Rules & History

A short history

Othello, originally known as Reversi, has captivated players for over a century with its strategic depth and simple rules. Its exact origin is disputed: two Englishmen, Lewis Waterman and John Mollett, each claimed to have invented the game in the 1880s, accusing one another of idea theft.

The game eventually faded into obscurity until the 1970s, when a Japanese salesman named Goro Hasegawa revived it. He standardized the starting position to create a more balanced, competitive experience, and renamed the game Othello — after Shakespeare's tragedy — for the dramatic reversals of fortune that mirror the play's shifting loyalties, and for the black-and-white pieces echoing its central conflict.

The rebranding was a masterstroke. Othello quickly gained popularity in Japan and then internationally, and is today played competitively worldwide.

How to play

  1. The board starts with four discs in the centre: two black on one diagonal, two white on the other.
  2. Black moves first. Players alternate turns.
  3. A legal move places one of your discs on an empty square so that it outflanks one or more of the opponent's discs — a straight line (in any of the eight directions) of opponent discs bounded at the far end by one of your own.
  4. All outflanked discs are flipped to your colour. Every move must flip at least one disc.
  5. If you have no legal move, you must pass; if neither player can move, the game ends.
  6. The player with the most discs when the board fills (or both players are stuck) wins.

A few tips

  • Corners can never be flipped — they anchor stable discs.
  • Beware the squares next to an empty corner (the X- and C-squares); taking them early often hands your opponent the corner.
  • Mobility matters: keeping more options than your opponent (and limiting theirs) is usually stronger than grabbing discs early.
